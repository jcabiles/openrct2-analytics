## North-star outcome
The audience is hiring managers, recruiters and interviewers for analytics-engineering and data-science roles. The front door is the website and the README (project owner, 2026-10-02).
- **Cold read (30 seconds and 5 minutes).** From the first screen, a first-time reader states what RollerCoaster Tycoon is and what the park company is; after 5 minutes, they state the company's rules and timeline, what analytics it needs and why, how the stack delivers it, and how the numbers are verified. Readers: a blind agent persona (recruiter, hiring manager) plus at least one real person. Baseline: fails (no site). Target: passes at v1.
- **Deep dive (30 minutes).** A technical reviewer traces one headline number from its game event through the contract, staging, intermediate and mart models to a dashboard, queries the warehouse directly in DuckDB, and runs the whole pipeline from the committed sample with one command. Baseline: fails. Target: passes at v1.
- **Guardrail: the numbers match the game.** Every number the site, README or a dashboard shows is tied to an in-game source by a dbt test, and a mismatch fails `make check` in CI. Baseline: no such tests. Target (project owner, 2026-10-18): every published number is tied to its in-game source at v1.1; v1 figures carry a "not yet reconciled" label.

## How work is run (project owner, 2026-10-02)
- **Research before building.** Wherever it makes sense, a lane starts with `/research`, ideation and a spike or pilot before any spec, so the project follows the practices, patterns and architectures real data teams and consultancies use. Each design decision becomes an ADR saying what was given up.
- **Verification labels, so checks that need the project owner never block overnight agents.** Every slice and ticket carries one:
  - `verify: agent` — CI and agents can check it completely.
  - `verify: owner-later` — agents build and review it; the project owner's check is queued in the owner queue (a list of pending checks with the time each takes, kept beside the ai-org log). Until it passes, the result is labeled provisional and nothing published claims it. A failed owner check reopens every slice built on that result.
  - `after:` — a line on an item naming the item or step that must finish before it starts; agents skip the item until then.
  - `waits-on-owner:` — a line on an item naming the step only the project owner can do (a decision, a real-play check, anything touching real saves or the old park backups, the clean start). Agents sort items by this line and skip them until the step is done; the same step is a row in the owner queue. One exception: an item named in the ready list below whose `waits-on-owner:` line covers only a check the project owner plays after the build is built by agents, and only that check waits.
- **Checks, speed tests and reviews (project owner, 2026-10-28, after a /pareto-scope run; detail in "Pareto scope (decided 2026-10-28)" at the end of this file).** One passing `make check` on a commit serves every later step on that same commit; a new commit runs it again. A code branch gets one Opus reviewer covering both angles plus the Codex review, one fix round and a fix check, and another round only for a blocking finding. A docs-only change gets one Sonnet reviewer and one fix round. A judged `make bench` passes under the noise rule: one tick over 10 ms in one run passes when the new code's share of that tick, read from that period's own time split, is under 10 ms and every mean is under 1.25 ms, with a `docs/FINDINGS.md` note; there are no re-runs over such a tick.
- **An integration branch, `develop`.** Agents merge a slice into `develop` only after its reviewer passes and `make check` is green; the project owner promotes `develop` to `main` in batches and still alone merges to `main`. The process lines in `CLAUDE.md` and `docs/ai-dlc/profile.md` say this (2026-10-02). Still to do (by the project owner): protection rules for `main` on GitHub.

## Business rules: the park company (project owner, 2026-09-27; timeline detail 2026-10-02)
- **The name: Theme Park Enterprise.** "Theme Park Enterprise" is the project's name for this way of playing the game: instead of one park, one company runs several parks on one shared calendar and reports them as a single business. The park company is that company. The rules below define it.
- **The company.** One company owns several pay-per-ride parks (entry is free; rides and stalls charge) and is reported like a real multi-park operator: by park and for the company, on one calendar.
- **A park's life.** The project owner builds one park at a time by hand (a managed park). (2026-11-15: two new parks may be filed from one play, and opening a park early gets a warning, not a block; NOW section "Player-friendly game for strangers". The published "Parks open one at a time" line in `docs/showcase/story/the-company-timeline.md` is reviewed in the site section's copy pass.) Once it is profitable and its scenario challenge is complete, the owner marks it ready for autopilot, and it switches at the next game-year boundary. It then runs headless (the game with no window and no player, the plugin still recording), one game year per run, triggered by the owner. Between runs the owner may refurbish it by hand, paused, or those days count as a managed session. A game year takes about 53 real minutes at normal speed and about 36 seconds at headless speed 8 without capture.- **The company timeline.** Park 1's first game day is **1 March 2000** on the company calendar. The managed park sets the company clock, and only one park is managed at a time. When a park goes to autopilot, the next park starts the following March: if Park 1 took exactly 3 game years (2000 to 2002), Park 2's first day is 1 March 2003, and Park 1's game year 4 is Park 2's game year 1, both company year 2003. Each park has two registry values: its company start year and its game-year origin (the game year it was in when its company life began: 1 for a park built from scratch, later for an old rehearsal save). Company year = company start year + (game year − game-year origin), with the game's month and day. Every autopilot park runs each company year the managed park runs, and stops at that year's end.
- **A season, not a calendar year.** A game year is 8 months, March to October, and is also the fiscal year; there is no November to February. Northern seasonal parks also close in winter, so this reads as realistic, but monthly and year-over-year reporting must show 8-month years.
- **Year-end close.** A company year's reports publish only after every park has finished that year. Autopilot parks never run past it.
- **Park identity.** The plugin mints a park ID once, when the park's storage has none, and every reload keeps it; loading an earlier save of the same park is a reload. A deliberate copy needs a new ID (NEXT 6 settles how). The project owner's try-out copies (duplicate saves used to try a rollercoaster design, then saved over the main save) keep the park ID; they are branches of the same park, not experiments.
- **Visitors, not retention.** Guests never return: leaving the park deletes them (OpenRCT2 v0.5.5 `Guest.cpp`). The metric is visitors per day and per month, which play the role of DAU and MAU for readers from tech.
- **The clean start.** Before v1 (project owner, 2026-10-16), after the dress rehearsal (NEXT 2c) passes, the project owner backs up, then clears, all saves and game data. One backed-up copy of the largest old park is kept outside the repo as the plugin benchmark's fixture. The company begins with the first park built after that.
